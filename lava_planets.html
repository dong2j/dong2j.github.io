Overview

‘‘…if it were land that ever burned
With solid, as the lake with liquid fire’’

— John Milton, Paradise Lost, Book I

Lava planets are rocky exoplanets that orbit so close to their stars, often in less than a day, that their surfaces become hot enough to melt rock. Most are expected to be tidally locked, with one side always facing the star. The day side may hold an ocean of magma, while the night side can cool and form a solid crust.

Well-known examples include 55 Cancri e, a planet about twice the size of Earth that orbits its Sun-like star in less than 18 hours at about 2000 K, and K2-141 b, which completes an orbit in less than 7 hours. Observations with the James Webb Space Telescope suggest that 55 Cancri e may have an atmosphere rich in CO or CO2, outgassed from and sustained by its magma ocean (Hu et al. 2024).

Because their interiors, surfaces and atmospheres are closely linked, lava planets are natural laboratories for studying how magma oceans and atmospheres exchange material, a process that also shaped the early Earth and other rocky planets.
