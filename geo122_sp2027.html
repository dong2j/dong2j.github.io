Description

This class is primarily intended as a first class for the Geosciences major, and it gives an overview of the Geosciences as they are practiced today. It can also serve as an introduction to the Geosciences for adjacent majors, such as atmospheric and climate science, and astronomy.

We open with the history of ideas about the Earth, to see how different theories of the Earth came to be conceptualized. Our efforts to understand the Earth highlight our relationship with the planet and help us understand our roles in the places where we live. We survey the subfields of geoscience, put forward by people from different backgrounds (geophysicists, geochemists, geologists, and climate scientists), each trying to understand the Earth with broad but sometimes limited field experience and observation. At the end, we zoom out to avoid any myopic view of our home planet and ask the question of the origins of Earth together with other planets in the solar system and the galaxy.

The class includes labs with maps and hand samples, and a group project toward the end.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you will:

  • Have a complete and in-depth survey of the Geosciences.

  • Be able to shop around the subfields of the Geosciences that interest you and pursue them further as a major.

  • And, most importantly, understand the Earth as a system. An outcrop could be the scar of a century-old earthquake, caused by the buckling of the lithosphere as it cools. The ultimate goal is for you to start to see the connections.

Assessment

Labs (35%): Weekly labs, plus a lab practical.
Exams (35%): Three midterm exams and a final exam.
Quizzes (20%): Six short in-class quizzes.
Group Project (10%): A group presentation in the last week of class.

Bonus (optional, up to 3%): Attend up to two designated departmental talks or events, announced in class. Each is worth up to 1.5 points: (1) a 300–500 word summary emailed as a PDF within two weeks (0.5 pt; required to present), covering the scientific question, the main evidence and conclusion, what you found most interesting and how it connects to the course, and one question for the speaker; (2) a two-slide, 3–5 minute presentation in class (1 pt). The summary is graded for completion; the presentation on whether you can explain, in your own words, what the speaker found and why it interested you.
